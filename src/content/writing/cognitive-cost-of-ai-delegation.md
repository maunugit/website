As I recently read about the release of the new Muse agent by Meta, I started to think about what it might look like as more and more people start using agentic AI. Muse seems to be especially targeted toward non-techies and people who previously have had very little idea of what AI could even be used for. It's specifically marketed to handle emails, or to do shopping for the user, both of which immediately brought up some thoughts about where this could be headed. 

The agent would have access to my inbox, could draft or automatically write and send responses, summarize the contents of messages, and so on. Obviously, if an agent handles my emails, I'm less involved in handling my emails. That is the point and one of the purposes of the tool. It's a helpful assistant and through each new update and feature they're getting more and more helpful. But how much help do I need? When I read and answer the emails I learn what is happening, decide what I think about things, and understand what I commit myself to. If I have an agent, where do these things go? Are they just delegated completely? Is it up to me to ensure that I'm still in the loop? Or should it be the agent's responsiblity? What would that even mean in terms of the behavior of the agent? 

Personally, I use AI to draft emails for me all the time. Usually I already know what I want to say, but quite often I actually notice something from the AI's response that is worth keeping. The final result is written by me, but the specific points and thoughts I could have missed are what I gained from the process. I consider this helpful. What truly bothers me is if I'm communicating about something meaningful, and I expect a human-authored answer, and instead receive a response that reads like an untouched ChatGPT answer, with none of the person's usual voice. This causes me to immediately wonder how much of my original message they actually even engaged with. Sometimes it almost feels insulting, and may cause me to stop reading their response altogether. In this case, it feels like assistance turns into substituting something I consider important and worth keeping. Let's imagine you meet a colleague who asks why you rejected their proposal yesterday, when you had automated your response. In the worst case, you might not even be familiar with the details of their proposal.

But like most things relating to LLMs, it's all relative. You can have a rough, untouched draft that gets carefully reviewed and polished into something that is nicer to read and gets your ideas across much better. I would consider this is helpful partial automation. You could have polished it yourself, and there are clear benefits from doing that regularly, but you chose to skip it and save some time. At the same time, you could have your agent imitate your voice perfectly while you remain completely uninvolved in the process. This would be full automation. In this case, if the email sounds exactly like my friend, but I would later know that they had never read either message, how should I feel about this? 

But is this anything new? Humans have delegated information and tasks for a very long time. The whole purpose of delegating is to relieve you from the work that you delegated, at least the majority of it. The task might be something you could do, but it could also be surprisingly tedious and time-consuming. I wouldn't mind using an agent to read through 70 pages of terms and conditions. Although, a lot of routine, mechanical work can enhance your own practice in judgement and the scope of your understanding on the matter. A software engineer develops this judgement partly through reviewing code, encountering bad practices, and figuring out what a better solution would look like. Some seemingly tedious work contains that practice. But it must be stated that the value does not necessarily come directly from doing it manually or repeatedly. I could struggle to understand a concept for hours when someone could have explained it to me in 5 minutes. 

Delegating information between humans has always required some level of synchronizing thoughts and opinions. An agent tasked with clearing my inbox might count the task as complete while leaving me out of the loop as to what it did exactly. Clearing an inbox has a relatively clear endpoint. Ensuring that I understand the decisions made along the way is harder to define, and even harder to verify. The agent can just report what it did, but what would count as sufficient evidence that I understood it? How much information would need to flow back, and how much would I need to engage with it, for the conversation to remain mine? I consider this particularly interesting, unresolved human-agent interface problem.

Perhaps an answer to this could be that the agent gives me a summary afterward. But would this put me back in the loop? I feel like there is a clear distinction between digesting a piece of information as it exists and digesting an AI summary of it. While the agent could successfully condense a longer text or multiple emails into a shorter summary, the result has a fundamentally different feel to it when I read it. Let's assume that it successfully managed to include all the important bits of information that convey the full meaning and intent of the original text. Still, while reading it, it produces another curious emotional feeling that I can only categorize as "disposable". It originates from the realization that I got it quickly and with minimal effort. I could regenerate the same summary indefinitely. I could tell the agent to summarize it again, summarize it differently, make it shorter, give me five bullet points, or just explain it to me like I'm 12 years old. It feels cheaper, almost fungible. Still, that feeling alone does not directly mean that I understand or remember less. But it does seem to change how I approach the material.

I don't seem to value a message solely for the information it contains. If I receive a handwritten letter, I regard it very differently than a printed copy of it, when both could contain the exact same sequence of words. If I expect a human response and suddenly get a fully AI-generated one, the difference in the regard I have for it is huge. That's not to say that all AI-generated text causes this. When I ask an AI a question directly, I don't have the same expectation of another person's attention. It will be interesting to then see what starts to happen when more and more generated language starts to circulate around communication systems. Perhaps, as generated content becomes more common, knowing that someone actually read and considered what I said will matter more to me. 

A big question (that someone should figure out an answer to quite soon) seems to be: "Which parts of human cognitive work should we actually automate?". If I now have an AI that makes me incredibly productive, but this does not require me to read, write, solve, or discuss difficult topics, can it still be labeled as productivity? Previously, the friction in producing that output was of value. Completing a task was meaningful because you did the work. Now you have tools that can complete most of the work you give it. In a sense, provided that you accept ownership of the result and can present it and explain it without much difficulty (having understood what it is and how it works), is it enough? But if I finish a task faster but become less capable of handling the next one myself, how should I interpret that tradeoff?

Perhaps a good personal agent is one that does not aim to minimize every single thing that I ever have to think about. One appealing promise of agentic AI was that it could remove the boring, mechanical work. But now that I think of it, that is a pretty ambiguous statement. What counts as boring or mechanical? First, I was told that I wouldn't need to do basic programming anymore. Then, they said that I wouldn't need to write complex software. Now Zuckerberg just told me that I don't even need to open my Gmail. 

Does the agent need to refuse a task? Should it disagree with me more? It seems slightly counter-intuitive to how it's designed. Its existence is to ultimately be a helpful assistant. But would it actually help me more if it pushed me to use my cognitive abilities? What if it regularly reminded me of things that are worth considering for myself, or had a rule where I should draft the email for it to then look over? A good agent might try to avoid silently substituting its understanding of my work and my life for my own. These all feel like questions about meaning, authority, trust, intention, and social norms. Or is this just something that would hinder us in moving forward, and that my agent should just do what it's told, no questions asked, as the capabilities of these agents keeps advancing? 

Maybe I want an assistant that occasionally tells me that I don't really need its help with a task. Whether I would appreciate that in the moment is another question. Perhaps some of the friction I want it to remove is also friction I would need it to preserve. It is a bit eerie to find myself asking for a more helpful version of: 
"I'm sorry Dave. I'm afraid I can't do that."
